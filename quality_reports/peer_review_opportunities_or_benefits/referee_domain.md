# Referee A Report — domain (CREDIBILITY disposition)

**Paper:** Opportunities or Benefits: Local Conditions and Refugee Labor Market Integration
**Target journal:** Review of Economic Studies (ReStud)
**Date:** 2026-05-18
**Referee:** A (domain) | Disposition: CREDIBILITY
**Critical peeve:** Identification assumption must be stated in one testable sentence.
**Constructive peeve:** Appreciates anticipated objections.

## Summary recommendation

**Major revision.** The paper has the bones of a contribution that could clear the ReStud bar — distinct two-dimensional variation (space and time, in both IVUR and IPBL) that no prior paper exploits jointly, large administrative panel, careful institutional grounding. But the identification section as currently written is not at ReStud level for a paper that calls itself "quasi-experimental." The single sentence stating the identifying assumption does not appear anywhere I can quote, the IPBL exogeneity discussion concedes confounding but offers no real test, and the design relies critically on a "haphazard" assignment claim that is asserted rather than demonstrated. The empirical work is solid; the identification narrative needs to be re-engineered before this paper is ready.

## One-paragraph summary of the paper (my read)

The authors exploit Austria's asylum-seeker dispersal policy combined with a sequence of state-level welfare reforms (2015–2017) to estimate the joint and interactive effects of two initial conditions at the moment of receiving protection — the local vacancy-to-unemployment ratio (IVUR, district × month) and the potential benefit level (IPBL, state × month × protection-type × family-status). Using administrative ASSD/BMAW data on 40k refugees who received protection between 2011 and August 2018, they regress monthly outcomes (employment, earnings, mobility, welfare receipt) on initial IVUR and IPBL with arrival-group (gender × quarter-of-arrival × initial-state × arrived-alone), month-year-of-protection, and district-of-protection fixed effects. Headline findings: an IQR increase in IVUR raises 12-month employment by ~4pp (~35% of mean) and persistently raises the probability of staying in the initial state; a €300 increase in IPBL reduces 12-month employment by ~1.5pp; the IPBL effect on employment is concentrated in tight labor markets (consistent with Dustmann et al. 2024); benefit effects on mobility persist while employment effects fade after ~2.5 years. The framing emphasizes that mobility responses reflect short-term adaptation rather than forward-looking labor-market optimization, undercutting Borjas's "greasing the wheels" mechanism for refugees.

## Strengths

1. **Two-dimensional variation is genuinely novel.** The paper's central methodological selling point — that prior work (Åslund-Rooth, Godøy, Azlor et al., Ferwerda) has variation in only one dimension while this paper has both spatial *and* temporal variation in both IVUR and IPBL — is real and lets them include district-of-protection fixed effects (Section 4.2.2, lines 76–79). This is a meaningful identification upgrade that the introduction sells appropriately.
2. **The vacancy data is a real comparative advantage.** Using v/u rather than unemployment alone, and being able to decompose by desired vs. seasonal occupations (Section 5a), is a level of granularity prior dispersal-policy papers cannot match. The seasonal-occupation result (Figure \ref{fig:seasonal_ivur}, lines 33–43 of `5a_mechanisms.tex`) is one of the cleverest things in the paper: if even idiosyncratic seasonal demand drives staying behavior, that's strong evidence the mechanism is immediate-constraint rather than forward-looking optimization.
3. **The interaction result is well-motivated and well-positioned.** Replicating Dustmann et al. (2024)'s benefit × labor-demand interaction in an entirely different institutional setting is a real contribution and the right way to use this data (Section 5.4, Table \ref{tab:interactions}). I would want the discussion to lean into this more.
4. **The constructive peeve is partially honored.** The authors anticipate the obvious political-economy confounder (FPÖ vote share, Section 5a) and acknowledge the resulting upper-bound interpretation for IPBL (line 79 of `4_method.tex`). The 35% private-accommodation ATT rescaling (Section 7, line 12) is also a nice ex-ante acknowledgment of treatment-intensity heterogeneity. The honest acknowledgment that FPÖ is a "bad control" for IVUR (line 68 of `5a_mechanisms.tex`) is admirable. **However, see MC2 — several other obvious objections are not anticipated.**

## Major concerns

### MC1: The identification assumption is never stated as one testable sentence

- **Lens:** Identification / CREDIBILITY peeve.
- **Issue:** Section 4.2.1 (`4_method.tex` lines 31–73) splits "identification" into two parts ("assignment to cells" and "exogeneity of IVUR/IPBL") but never compresses them into a single conditional independence statement that a referee could critique. The closest the paper comes is line 35: *"conditional on the arrival group, denoted as $\delta_{g}$ in our specifications, asylum-seekers find themselves quasi-randomly assigned to the district where they live when their protection is granted."* But this is not the identifying assumption for the *whole* design — it's the assignment assumption. The exogeneity assumption for IPBL is hedged ("we deem it unlikely... the argument is less clear for IPBL... we argue that we can determine the direction of a potential bias") with no testable counterpart. The reader is left to assemble the assumption from a paragraph that mixes assertions about behavior ("asylum-seekers cannot select a district based on perceived labor market prospects") with conditioning sets. A ReStud referee cannot accept a paper where the identifying restriction has to be reverse-engineered from prose.
- **Why this matters:** This is a two-treatment design with quite different assignment mechanisms — IVUR varies at the district × month-of-protection level (effectively determined by *when* the bureaucracy processes a claim within a placement), IPBL varies at the state × month × protection-type × family-status level (a mix of cross-state policy variation and reform-induced time variation). The conditional independence assumptions for these two are *different*, and the paper conflates them. Specifically: IVUR identification leans on the timing-of-processing being uncorrelated with the asylum-seeker's labor-market potential conditional on arrival group and district FE; IPBL identification leans on reform timing being uncorrelated with unobserved state-level shocks affecting refugee outcomes. The paper needs to write each one out.
- **What would change my mind:** A single paragraph in Section 4.2 that states, in one sentence per treatment: *"Conditional on [exact FE set], we assume $\mathbb{E}[\epsilon \mid \text{IVUR}, X] = \mathbb{E}[\epsilon \mid X]$"* and *"Conditional on [exact FE set], we assume $\mathbb{E}[\epsilon \mid \text{IPBL}, X] = \mathbb{E}[\epsilon \mid X]$"*. Then, for each, a paragraph listing the threats and an explicit pointer to the test that addresses each threat. As of now, line 35 and line 77 do this work informally for IVUR; nothing does this work for IPBL beyond hand-waving about an "upper bound."

### MC2: The "haphazard within arrival group" claim is asserted, not demonstrated

- **Lens:** Identification.
- **Issue:** The core assignment claim (`4_method.tex` line 35; `2_background.tex` line 14) is that within {gender × quarter-of-arrival × initial-state × arrived-alone}, allocation to municipalities/districts depends only on "the next available housing unit." The evidence offered for this is (i) interviews with "multiple experts" (footnote in `2_background.tex` line 14), (ii) the balance regression in Table \ref{tab:ivur_ipbl_balance}, and (iii) the assertion itself. None of this is sufficient for ReStud. The balance table (which I read at `tables/treatment_characteristics_corr.tex`) is on **observables only** and even there several coefficients survive with stars: secondary education on IVUR is significant at 10% even with arrival-group FE (col 2: −1.849*, SE 1.084); secondary education on IPBL is −4.257* even with arrival-group FE (col 4); B/C German skills on IPBL is −4.049** (col 4). The paper waves these away as "negligible in magnitude" (line 71) and points to the Wald test p-value of 0.594. But (a) Wald-test joint significance is not the relevant test against selection-on-unobservables — it tests sorting on the included controls only; (b) the magnitudes are not actually negligible relative to the SD of IPBL (€317) — a 4-euro difference is small, but the systematic pattern that higher-educated refugees face lower IPBL is exactly the direction in which a selection story would predict. Most importantly, **balance is on baseline characteristics; there is no analog test on pre-protection outcomes** (e.g., pre-protection employment, training participation), and crucially, **the previous draft contained pre-trend figures that are now commented out** (`6_robustness.tex` lines 83–100, `fig:rob_labor_mobility_pre_trends`).
- **Why this matters:** The whole design rests on this assertion. A CREDIBILITY referee cannot take the haphazard-within-cell claim on faith when (i) the paper explicitly acknowledges municipalities can "express preferences" (line 14 of `2_background.tex`), (ii) the asylum process duration varies 12–16 months and is one of the dimensions over which IVUR's time variation is generated, and (iii) the previously-existing pre-trend evidence has been removed from the manuscript. The fact that a previous version had pre-trend figures and they are gone is, for a CREDIBILITY referee, a red flag.
- **What would change my mind:** Three things, in order of importance: (a) **Restore the pre-trend figures or replacement.** Refugees have very low formal employment before protection (the paper itself notes this), but the design has a richer pre-period than the paper exploits — language-course participation, PES contact, basic-needs receipt before protection, even within-asylum-process moves all exist in the data. Showing that IVUR and IPBL realized *at the (future) date of protection* are uncorrelated with these pre-period outcomes among individuals who haven't yet received protection would be a real test of the design. (b) **Direct evidence on the within-arrival-group allocation rule.** The "expert interview" footnote is too thin. If the BBA assignment data exists, even an anonymized example of how a particular arrival cohort was split across municipalities (with the order of placement compared to capacity-available timing) would be far stronger. (c) **Drop or formally argue against the surviving balance coefficients.** Either the secondary-education and B/C-German signs are coincidental (and a randomization-inference-style test under the null of conditional random assignment would say so) or they reflect residual sorting that pollutes the estimates. Either show the former or bound the latter.

### MC3: The IPBL identification is conceded to be confounded — and the "upper bound" argument doesn't bind

- **Lens:** Identification / CREDIBILITY.
- **Issue:** `4_method.tex` lines 77–79 explicitly admit: *"The argument is less clear for IPBL. The welfare reforms, which drive most of the variation in benefit levels, might result from a broader change in attitudes towards refugees, which might also affect the outcomes. While we cannot fully rule out this possibility, we argue that we can determine the direction of a potential bias. Reductions in benefits likely reflect a more negative attitude, which might in itself trigger relocation to another state. Thus, the estimated effect for benefits would capture this as well. Our estimate for the effect of benefits on relocation thus reflects an upper bound (in absolute terms)."* This is a partial defense at best. First, the upper-bound argument is plausible for the *mobility* outcome but not for the *employment* outcome — a more anti-refugee environment plausibly reduces refugee employment directly (employer discrimination, harassment), which would *amplify* the negative IPBL→employment effect estimated by the same mechanism, not bound it. The paper does not address this asymmetry. Second, the FPÖ-share robustness in `5a_mechanisms.tex` (Figure \ref{fig:fpo}) is treated as confirming the design, but FPÖ vote share is itself an attitude proxy and may absorb the very confounder of concern; this is exactly the kind of test that "passing" tells you very little. Third, three of the four welfare reforms documented in Appendix B (Upper Austria 2016, Lower Austria 2017, Burgenland 2017) were **subsequently overturned by courts** — a fact the paper notes (`appendix.tex` lines 328–331) but does not exploit. The overturning creates a clean within-state reversal that could anchor an event-study or DD design.
- **Why this matters:** The IPBL results are the welfare-magnet finding the paper sells. If a ReStud referee leaves the paper unsure that IPBL is exogenous conditional on the FE, the welfare-magnet contribution is gone.
- **What would change my mind:** (a) An event-study around each of the four major reforms (Lower Austria 2016, Upper Austria 2016, Lower Austria 2017, Burgenland 2017) — including the **reversal** in Upper Austria 2018 and Lower Austria/Burgenland 2018 — would let me see whether the effect is symmetric across cut-and-restore. A reform that is later judicially reversed gives the cleanest possible test that the result isn't attitude-driven, because the attitude change is sticky but the policy change reverses. The paper hints at this in `1_intro.tex` (line 51) and `appendix.tex` (line 331) but doesn't execute it. (b) A formal placebo: re-estimate IPBL effects on outcomes for *natives* (or for established immigrants) in the same district × time cells. These groups face zero treatment but the same attitude environment. If the placebo is null, the upper-bound argument tightens substantially. (c) An explicit reckoning with the asymmetry between mobility and employment outcomes in the bias-sign argument.

### MC4: Inference at state-year-of-protection clustering rests on 9 states × 8 years = 72 clusters, and the wild-bootstrap robustness shows confidence intervals widen materially

- **Lens:** Identification / inference.
- **Issue:** Standard errors are clustered at state-year-of-protection (Section 6, `6_robustness.tex` line 15). The paper notes that state-only clustering would be problematic because Austria has 9 states. State-year gives 72 clusters, which is acceptable but the bootstrap robustness (Figure \ref{fig:errors_all}) is glossed over — the text concedes that wild-bootstrap CIs "widen relative to our main specification" but doesn't show the reader by how much for the headline estimates, nor whether the IPBL effect (which is the smaller and more contested of the two) survives wild bootstrap at conventional levels. Given that IPBL identification is driven by a small number of *state-level* reforms (4 reforms across 3 states), the effective number of independent treatment shocks for the IPBL coefficient is closer to 4 than to 72. This is the classic problem of clustering at a level finer than the level of treatment variation \citep{abadie2017should, mackinnon2023}.
- **Why this matters:** If the IPBL effect on employment loses significance under state-clustered wild bootstrap, the welfare-magnet contribution is materially weaker than the paper claims.
- **What would change my mind:** A table (not a figure) of the headline coefficients with wild-bootstrap p-values under (i) state-of-protection clustering, (ii) state-year clustering, (iii) state-of-protection clustering with state-year fixed effects. Specifically I want to see whether the 12-month IPBL→employment coefficient survives state-level wild bootstrap. If the answer is "barely," the paper should say so. If "not," the framing of the welfare effect should reflect that the identification really rests on the small handful of reforms.

### MC5: The "what does this teach us about the world" claim is overclaimed in one specific direction — the no-greasing-the-wheels result

- **Lens:** Substance / Contribution.
- **Issue:** The introduction (line 17) and conclusion frame the paper as overturning Borjas's (2001) "greasing the wheels" story for refugees: *"the combination of dispersal policies with subsequent unrestricted mobility does not contribute to 'greasing the wheels' of the labor market."* This is an important headline claim — it's the kind of statement that justifies a ReStud-level contribution. But the evidence for it is indirect. What the paper actually shows is: (a) refugees move when they face weak labor markets at the initial location, but (b) they overwhelmingly move *to Vienna*, which has weak labor demand. The paper concludes from this that movers are not labor-market-optimizing. But an equally valid reading is that refugees are optimizing over a *different* objective — say, place utility, networks, or, as the paper's own discussion (`7_discussion.tex` line 6) notes, housing — that is endogenously correlated with the metro size of Vienna. The seasonal-occupation result (which I praised in Strengths) provides better evidence for the short-term-constraint story, but it's about why people *stay*, not why people *move to Vienna*. The greasing-the-wheels claim conflates these.
- **Why this matters:** ReStud cares whether you can defend the central intellectual claim. If the headline finding is "refugees don't grease the wheels," that needs to be defended against the obvious "they're optimizing over network/housing/amenity considerations that the model omits" alternative. The paper currently doesn't.
- **What would change my mind:** Either (a) restrict the claim to: "refugees' relocation choices respond to short-term labor demand at *origin* but not to expected labor demand at *destination*" — which is what the data actually show and is sufficient for the contribution — or (b) provide direct evidence on what movers' destinations look like in terms of *expected* refugee labor demand vs. networks/amenities, separately. As stated, the greasing-the-wheels narrative is a leap.

### MC6: The interaction result (Table \ref{tab:interactions}) — the most novel substantive finding — is under-developed

- **Lens:** Contribution / Substance.
- **Issue:** The interaction result (IPBL effect on employment is concentrated in tight labor markets) is sold as one of the paper's three main contributions (`1_intro.tex` line 19, line 27). Table \ref{tab:interactions} shows the marginal effects at IVUR = 0, mean, and mean+0.2. But these are pinned with state-year-of-protection clustering, and as flagged in MC4, the IPBL variation is much coarser. More substantively: the paper says (`5_results.tex` line 128) the finding is "in line with" Dustmann et al. (2024). But Dustmann et al. is the headline benchmark paper this contribution converses with. ReStud will want to see *what your interaction tells us that Dustmann's doesn't* — different institutional setting, yes, but the same direction. Is the magnitude comparable? Different in interpretable ways? The conclusion (line 17) just reports the result without taking a stand. This is the kind of result where ReStud expects a paragraph of theory: under what model of refugee labor supply (search-with-reservation-wage, household labor supply with two earners, etc.) is the cross-partial negative? The "ADD SOME DISCUSSION ABOUT THEORY HERE" placeholder at `5_results.tex` line 112 confirms this hasn't been done.
- **Why this matters:** This is your most distinctive finding. The contribution is not "we replicated Dustmann in Austria"; it's "we replicate Dustmann, and here's what the joint pattern teaches us about refugee labor supply." The paper doesn't tell us what we learn.
- **What would change my mind:** A short conceptual paragraph (does not have to be a model) at the top of Section 5.4 stating which class of behavioral models is consistent with a negative cross-partial of IPBL × IVUR on employment, ruling out one or two alternatives. Plus a comparison of magnitudes to Dustmann et al. in interpretable units (e.g., the implied labor-supply elasticity at different labor-demand levels).

## Minor concerns

1. **Abstract.** "These effects persist for about 2.5 years" — but the IPBL→mobility effects persist for 60 months, not 2.5 years. The abstract makes the temporal asymmetry between employment and mobility effects sound smaller than the rest of the paper claims it is. Rewrite for accuracy.
2. **`1_intro.tex` line 27.** "Neither labor demand nor benefits affect the probability of leaving Austria altogether." Good. But this is buried; given the immigration-policy literature reads "leaving Austria" as a key first-order outcome, lift it into the abstract.
3. **`3_data.tex` line 8.** "In Appendix \ref{subsec:pes_selection}, we test whether the number of refugees in our data... correlates with our main explanatory variables, which is not the case." Then `appendix.tex` line 238 actually says: *"the effect of the IVUR becomes statistically significant at the 5% level... our estimates for the effect of IVUR on employment might be upward biased."* Reconcile.
4. **Section 5.1 wage result.** The null on log monthly wages (Figure \ref{fig:economic_results}, panel c, line 22) is conditional on employment and the paper concedes the selection problem. But this result is doing real work — it's what lets you attribute earnings effects to extensive-margin employment changes. Either run a Heckman-style bound or formally acknowledge that the wage result is descriptive at best.
5. **Section 5.6 ("Relevant initial labor market") Table \ref{tab:ivuripbl_arrival}.** "The point estimate on employment is roughly halved and loses statistical significance" when controlling for arrival-time IPBL. This is a non-trivial finding that should be flagged in the abstract/intro as a caveat: the IPBL→employment result is sensitive to the choice of *when* to measure the benefit shock. Currently this is buried in a robustness subsection.
6. **`5a_mechanisms.tex` line 4 still reads `%footnote{The analysis in this section is preliminary and the measure for desired occupations is currently refined.}`** — commented out but the text suggests work in progress. Confirm the desired-occupation measure is finalized before resubmission.
7. **Citation to Hangartner.** Multiple comments in `1_intro.tex` (lines 86, 96) reference "Hangartner et al 2023" but I don't see this in the live text. Either include or drop.
8. **`6_robustness.tex`.** Several robustness exercises are described in text but only have appendix figures — consider an in-text robustness summary table.
9. **`8_conclusion.tex` line 5** still has a stray paragraph fragment about subsidiary-protected coverage in the conclusion file — appears to be cut/paste residue.
10. **Policy simulation (`7_discussion.tex` lines 14–22).** The counterfactual exercise assumes treatment effects are constant in IPBL — this is an extrapolation outside support for some scenarios. Acknowledge.

## What this paper teaches me about the world (ReStud bar)

If the design holds up, this paper teaches me three things: (1) refugee mobility within a free-movement regime responds to *initial* local conditions in ways that look like adaptation to immediate constraints, not forward-looking labor-market optimization — because they predominantly move to a city with weak labor demand and benefits are doing a fair amount of the work; (2) employment effects of initial labor demand are large but not persistent, so the "scarring" intuition from native labor-market-entry research doesn't carry to refugees in this setting — likely because mobility is the safety valve; (3) the welfare-employment trade-off is conditional on local labor-market tightness, replicating Dustmann et al. (2024) outside Denmark. The first finding is the most novel. But for me to confidently take it home, the identification of the IPBL effect needs to be tightened (MC3) and the "no greasing the wheels" framing needs to be either narrowed or better defended (MC5). On the current version, I learn something — but not as much as the introduction promises. A serious revision could clear the ReStud bar; this draft does not.
